The Mentor Mindset

At the most basic level the mentor's mindset is holding high standards while providing high support.

In practice it comes from beliefs and manifests as behaviors, interventions, and actions that give respect, hold the standard high, and provide the support and resources needed to meet or exceed those high standards.

Required beliefsFive

As David Yeager says:

Your beliefs about young people shape your style. If you think they're unruly, you become an enforcer. If you think they're fragile, you become a protector. But if you believe they have great potential under the right conditions, you become a mentor. David Yeager, 10 to 25
Belief 01
They are capable, and they deserve respect
The neurobiological incompetence model — teen brains are simply underdeveloped — was argued by the MacArthur network to spare juveniles the death penalty and life without parole. A legal strategy became a scientific consensus. The alternative account is Ron Dahl's, and the National Academies' The Promise of Adolescence (2019).
Belief 02
Status and respect drive them from 10 to 25
Social status and prestige are what mattered most for long-term survival in group life, so young minds are very focused on fitting in. The status–respect mismatch is the gap between the standing they neurologically need and the standing their environment actually gives them. Most of the trouble lives in that gap.
Belief 03
Hormones make them ready to change, not incapable
The Vegemite study: young adults asked to eat an unpleasant supplement complied 66% of the time when asked respectfully and 47% when asked dismissively. Given extra testosterone they were the most compliant when respected (68%) and the least when disrespected (32%). Birds that don't go through puberty never learn a mating song, and never reproduce.
Belief 04
Anything left unsaid gets guessed at
Across a power gap there is a barrier of mistrust, and whatever you don't say gets filled in by the person with less power. The guess runs negative, usually way negative. Silence is not neutral.
Belief 05
Beliefs guide behavior
Someone who believes prejudice can be reduced by education has more cross-group conversations. Someone who doesn't, avoids them. Change what a person believes is possible and the actions follow on their own — which is why four of the nineteen techniques target a belief rather than a behaviour.

07 · The four moves of a respectful request

ThenMake a respectful request — ask, honor their status, validate, presume agency.

What it looks like

You are about to assign it, and you can already hear how that lands.

Say this

Opening — say who you are and why you're here
“Hey, I'm James, I'm one of the guides here. I wanted to talk to you about the next unit.”

1 · Ask, don't tell
“I wanted to ask whether you'd consider taking the placement test this week.”

2 · Honor their status, don't invoke yours
“You've been running your own schedule all semester, so I figured I'd just explain why this matters rather than assign it.”

Middle — name the hard part before they do
“Some people find this test frustrating because it puts you back on things you thought you were done with.”

3 · Validate and explain, don't diminish
“You might find it helps to think of that frustration as the part where you find out exactly what's still shaky, which is the whole point.”

4 · Presume agency
“Thanks for considering it. Let me know what you decide.”

Why

This is Yeager's Figure 1.3, the respectful and disrespectful scripts from the Vegemite study set side by side. Respectful got 66% compliance, dismissive 47%; with extra testosterone the split widened to 68% and 32%. Every move turns on one small word — consider, might, helps, decide. On the fourth move Yeager adds: acknowledge they'll make up their own mind, then make clear you're rooting for the good choice, and point out that their actions carry consequences beyond themselves.

Enforcer

Protector
“Only if you want to — no pressure either way.”

ThenBelieve the fear and keep the demand — then ask what would make it possible.

What it looks like

Say this

1 · Believe the fear

“You're still presenting Thursday. What would make it possible?”

04 · Give responsibility

ThenGive responsibility — hand over a piece of your own support work, one EDGE step at a time.

What it looks like

Nothing is wrong. They have been reliable for weeks — which is exactly why it is easy to leave them exactly where they are.

Do this

Not a sentence — a hand-over. Give them a piece of your support work, the part you have been doing on their behalf, and keep giving away more as each piece holds.

Explain
Say what the piece is and why it matters, so they know what they're taking on.

Demonstrate
Do it once in front of them, talking through what you're doing and why.

Guide
They do it, you're beside them. Help when asked. Let them repeat it until it's theirs.

Enable
Step back and watch. This is the rung most adults never get to, and it is the only one that changes who owns the outcome.

Then start again one level up. At Camp Champions, the players don't attend the basketball camp and help out — they lead it.

Why

There are three rungs — respect their autonomy, presume their agency, then give them responsibility — and a student doing consistently good work has outgrown the first two. The common objection is that this costs you time. Yeager's publisher puts it the other way round: through back-and-forth conversations young people feel empowered and managers transfer responsibility, so the people using the mentor mindset end up with more time, not less. The four steps above are the Boy Scouts' EDGE method — Explain, Demonstrate, Guide, Enable — which is the same hand-over written as a procedure.

Not this

Enforcer
Gives them more work at the same level of supervision. Harder, not freer.
Protector
Leaves them where they are succeeding, because it is going well and why risk it.

07 · Ask, don't tell — Jen's four questions

ThenAsk, don't tell — run the four questions and let them talk themselves into a real plan.

What it looks like

A plan that obviously won't work, and you can see exactly why.

Say this

“What are you planning to work on today?”

“Which part of that matters most right now?”

“Do you have what you need to do that part?”

“If I got you that, how much could you finish today?”

“Try it and come find me if you get stuck.”

Why

Jen met Yeager for fifteen minutes every morning of a twelve-week internship. He arrived each day with an unrealistic plan and she never once corrected it — he talked himself into a real one every time. He finished his list by 3pm daily and the project weeks early, and calls what she gave him a manager in the head. His own read on why it worked: the questions sounded curious and authentic rather than judgmental. Instead of getting angry, get curious.

06 · Prohibition raises the status of the thing

ThenDon't prohibit it — show them who profits and let them decide.

What it looks like

Vaping, or whatever you were about to forbid.

Say this

“The companies making those hired the same people who sold cigarettes to your grandparents. They picked the flavors specifically for people your age. Do you want to be their easiest customer?”

Why

Telling youth no often increases the limited thing — the classic “say no to drugs” programmes raised usage. Prohibition hands the behaviour status, and status is the thing they are optimising for. 08 · Transparency statement

ThenOpen with a transparency statement — say your intention before anything else.

What it looks like

They can see you coming and they don't know why, so they're already guessing.

Say this

The shape
“Hi — ___ is my intention here.”

Before a hard conversation
“Before we start — my intention here is to figure out what's getting in the way, not to get you in trouble. I'm not writing anything down.”

Why

Kyle Dobson's policing study: officers who approached civilians with no explanation left 70% of them feeling threatened. The same officers, opening with one sentence of intent — “I'm walking around trying to get to know the community” — had conversations twice as long, far more positive, and built real rapport. 10 · Treaties in the war of meaning

ThenMake a treaty, not a truce — run the six questions.

What it looks like

The third time this month, and it always ends the same way.

Say this

Their meaning
“What does this mean to you?”
“How is that meaning making you feel?”
“Is that serving your goals?”

Another meaning
“What is something else it could mean?”
“How would it make you feel if you thought that?”
“Would that serve your goals?”

Why

From Lorena Seidel. Make treaties, not truces — a truce just ends the fighting. 11 · Stress reappraisal

ThenDo stress re-appraisal — the pounding heart is the body getting them ready, not a warning.

What it looks like

Racing heart, shallow breath, ten minutes before it starts.

Say this

“Your heart pounding right now — that's not a sign you're going to fail. That's your body pushing more oxygen to your brain. It's getting you ready. People who feel that usually do better, not worse.”

Why

Note what is not said: calm down. The arousal stays exactly where it is; what changes is what they think it means — a threat response becomes a challenge response. And the claim is true, which is why it survives a student who is sceptical of pep talks. Jamieson et al. 2010: GRE maths 705 → 770. Jamieson et al. 2016, 93 community-college developmental maths students across five semesters — and the control group there was told to ignore their stress, which is the comparison reappraisal beat. In stressful situations demands exceed resources, so the mentor raises the resources rather than lowering the standard.

ThenRun synergistic mindsets — validate, then a growth reframe, then stress re-appraisal.

17 · The belonging story, in four beats

ThenHand them a previous student's belonging story, in four beats.

What it looks like

“Everyone else already knows how this works.” They go quiet rather than ask.

Say this

You don't say it — a previous student does, in writing, and this one reads it.

1 · Struggle is normal
“My first few weeks I thought I was the only one who didn't get it. Everyone else seemed to already know how the whole thing worked, and I didn't want to say anything and look like I didn't belong.”

2 · Change was possible
“Then I noticed my guide asked questions instead of just telling people they were wrong, and I figured maybe not knowing was allowed here.”

3 · What they actually did
“So I started saying when I was stuck. I asked another student to walk me through the app. I started going to my guide with what I'd already tried instead of waiting to be asked.”

4 · Snowball
“By the spring I was the one other people came to. It's still hard. It's just not lonely anymore.”

Why

Competence and belonging are tightly bound: normalize, then change is possible, then action steps, then a snowball — and a happy ending that doesn't promise the struggle ends. Yeager's Figure 9.4 annotates a real UT student's version, and the instructive part is the proportion: struggle takes roughly half the story, the pivot is a single sentence, and the action steps are three ordinary requests for help. Collect 3–8 of these from interviews with previous students. Walton & Cohen 2011, Science, N = 92 — the Black–white GPA gap cut 52% over three years, from one hour.

18 · People can change

ThenChange the belief that character is fixed — people can change.

What it looks like

Someone bullied or excluded them, and they are still carrying it.

Say this

Tell the story first: an adult runs into the person who bullied them in high school. The bully has changed — they regret it, they still think about it, and they ask to be understood as someone who did it in a misguided attempt to gain status. That encounter lets the adult realise people can change, and the hatred and shame start turning into something closer to pity, which doesn't consume them.

Then the four steps
1. Adolescence is a time of seeking status and respect, and that drive leads some young people to do harmful things. Bullying is a status move, not evidence of character.
2. Kids who bully need to learn different ways of behaving — a missing skill, not a fixed trait.
3. As the brain matures they learn to change, often by finding more productive outlets for the same appetite for status, like leadership. The drive doesn't go away; it gets redirected.
4. People who are shy and have few friends can also change, once they feel comfortable. So even if some peers exclude them now, they can still have a good social life later.

Why

It never asks anyone to forgive anyone. It changes one belief — that character is fixed — and lets the emotion follow. Hatred requires believing the person is permanently that way; remove the belief and the emotion has nowhere to sit. And watch step 4, which turns the whole thing around: steps 1–3 are about the person who hurt them, step 4 is about them. If character isn't fixed, that applies to their own social standing too — which is the part they were actually worried about.

Enforcer
“Get over it, it was years ago.”
Protector
“You're right, they're a terrible person.”

ThenRun a purpose intervention — and they write it, not you.

What it looks like

They're settled, they're fine socially, and they still won't work.

19 · Inclusive excellence

ThenInclusive excellence — hold the bar exactly where it is and raise the support.

What it looks like

They're behind, they know it, and the kind thing looks like an easier version.

Say this

“The bar doesn't move for you. What moves is how much support you get to reach it. Here's what's available, and here's how much of it I think you should take.”

08Transparency“Hi, ___ is my intention here.” Explain why you're giving feedback, don't just give it.

Kyle Dobson's policing study: officers who approached civilians with no explanation left 70% feeling threatened. The same officers opening with “I'm walking around trying to get to know the community” had conversations twice as long, far more positive, and built real rapport. This is what gets you over the barrier of mistrust.

If approaching a kid, or opening a hard conversation
10Creating treaties in the war of meaningSix questions in two halves — their meaning, then an alternative one.

From Lorena Seidel. What does this mean to you? How is that meaning making you feel? Is that serving your goals? Then: What is something else it could mean? How would it make you feel if you thought that? Would that serve your goals?

A treaty, not a truce: both sides assess needs, both are aware of what the other needs in order to thrive, both mutually agree the terms, and either can renegotiate with the lines of communication kept open. “The generational divide is a war of meaning.”

16Mix of motivesLearn all the motives, not the first one they hand you. “What else matters to you here?”

As in Adam Grant's Give and Take, the greatest winners in life hold others' goals and feelings high and do the same with their own, then find answers meeting both requirements. Kids want this too. Warren Buffett's “what else?” is the same instrument in a negotiation.

Meaning and purpose

13Connect to meaning and purpose now, not in the futureMake broad arguments, not narrow ones. And don't cause anxiety while encouraging purpose.

Study hard for a future job, versus study history hard because you're interviewing someone interesting. Instead of “you may use maths in a sales or finance job,” try “maths and writing make your brain stronger, which helps you improve the world.” Purpose is not an all-or-nothing proposition and shouldn't be delivered as one.

14Values-alignment interventionsAlign the behaviour with autonomy, status and fairness — what they already value — not a future benefit.

Make the adult the ally against a manipulator rather than the manipulator. The study version had students graffiti fast-food posters to make them actually true statements: high status plus rebellion against control. Bryan, Yeager, Hinojosa et al. 2016, PNAS 113(39) — double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled, 536 eighth-graders, the entire eighth grade of a Texas middle school.

15Purpose interventionsThe student writes it. Belonging answers “do I fit here”; purpose answers “why does this matter”.

Students identify a problem in the world they care about, read short accounts from other students describing how caring about something beyond themselves made them work harder, then write their own version addressed to future students. Running the wrong one — belonging when they needed purpose — wastes the intervention.

Yeager, Henderson, Paunesku, Walton, D'Mello, Spitzer & Duckworth 2014, JPSP 107(4). Self-transcendent purpose: d = 0.21 for low performers, 0.06 and not significant for high performers.

Belief change

17Competence is very related to belongingNormalize → change is possible → action steps → snowball. Four beats, written by a previous student.

Struggle is normal, so they are not alone or uniquely deficient. An example of how change was possible. What actions the student took once they saw it. How those steps snowballed — a happy ending that doesn't promise the struggle ends.

Yeager's Figure 9.4 (p. 242) annotates a real UT student's example with the four beats marked in the margin, and the instructive part is the proportion: struggle takes roughly half the story, the pivot is a single sentence, and the action steps are three ordinary requests for help — a lab mate, a friend, office hours. Collect 3–8 such stories from interviews with previous students, then edit them into the right order. Walton & Cohen 2011, Science, N = 92; the Black–white GPA gap cut 52% over three years.

18People can changeA separate intervention from the belonging story, though they sit close together in the chapter. p. 248.

What they did

High schoolers were told a story about an adult who ran into someone who had bullied them back in high school. The former bully had changed — they regretted it, admitted they still think about it, and asked to be understood as someone who had done it out of a misguided attempt to gain status. The story then explained how that chance encounter helped the adult realise that people can change, and how that realisation let them process what they had been carrying; the hatred and shame started turning into something closer to pity, which didn't consume them.

Then the science of how people change

1. Adolescence is a time of seeking status and respect, and that drive can lead some young people to do harmful things. Bullying gets explained as a status move, not as evidence of character.
2. Kids who bully need to learn different ways of behaving. The problem is framed as a missing skill.
3. As the brain matures, young people learn to change their behaviour — for example by finding more productive outlets for the same appetite for status, such as leadership. The drive doesn't go away; it gets redirected.
4. People who are shy and have few friends can also change. They can learn to be more social once they feel comfortable, so even if some peers exclude them now, they can still have a good social life later.

Step 4 turns the intervention around. Steps 1–3 are about the person who hurt them; step 4 is about the student themselves — if character isn't fixed, that applies to their own social standing too, which is the part they are actually worried about.

What it's doing

It isn't asking anyone to forgive anyone. It changes one belief — that character is fixed — and lets the emotion follow. Hatred requires believing the person is permanently that way. Remove the belief and the emotion has nowhere to sit.

Stress and the standard

11Stress re-appraisalDemands exceed resources, so raise the resources. Never “calm down”.

Threat response versus challenge response. The arousal stays exactly where it is; what changes is what the student thinks it means — and the claim is true, which is why it survives a student sceptical of pep talks.

Jamieson, Mendes, Blackstock & Schmader 2010, “Turning the knots in your stomach into bows,” JESP 46(1), 208–212 — GRE maths 705 → 770. Jamieson, Peters, Greenwood & Altose 2016, SPPS — 93 community-college developmental maths students across five semesters, where the control group was told to ignore their stress. See also the TSST, the Trier Social Stress Test.

If a student is stressed, and worried about being stressed
12Synergistic mindsetsGrowth mindset and stress reappraisal together. Neither alone does much.

Teach that ability can grow without teaching that the strain of growing it is useful, and the difficulty reads as proof they can't. Yeager's Box 7.6 is a full worked script — a student asks whether they're good enough for college and the reply moves through validation, then a growth reframe, then stress reappraisal, with each move labelled inline. Yeager, Bryan, Gross, Murray, Krettek Cobb, Santos, Graveling, Johnson & Jamieson 2022, Nature 607(7919), 512–520.

19Inclusive excellenceMore opportunity to get better, never a lower bar. p. 258.

Uri Treisman — Berkeley calculus. He found Black students weren't failing from lack of ability or motivation but from studying alone, and built structured group work harder than the regular course. More difficulty plus more company, not less difficulty.

Keivan Stassun — the Fisk–Vanderbilt Master's-to-PhD Bridge Program, which became the largest US producer of Black and Latino PhDs in physics and astronomy with no lowered admissions standard. He has been published in Nature thirteen times.
