Sales training

TPC

Talks · Presentations · Closes. Five steps, and a count of how far you got.

One rule, over all five

End with a question mark.

Every step below finishes with a question. A statement hands them nothing to answer, so they nod and keep walking. A question makes them respond, and a response is a sale still alive.

You are on the pier with a box of candy and a cooler. Here is the proper process.

1The Greeting

What is a greeting?

“Hi, how are you?”

That is the whole thing. It is not clever and it is not a pitch. You are finding out whether this person will talk to you at all.

Three things matter more than the words. What are they?

SEE

SSmile. Before you speak, not after.
EEye contact. If you are looking at the box, they will walk past the box.
EEnthusiasm. Nobody buys from someone who sounds like they would rather be elsewhere.

You will say “Hi, how are you?” a hundred times today. SEE is the only part that changes between the ones that work and the ones that don't.

They said “good, you?” — now how do you get to the sale?

You bridge. Two or three words, and they buy you a few more seconds:

“Quick question—”

“Real quick—”

The transition isn't a step of its own. It is how you get out of the greeting without an awkward gap, and it goes straight into step 2.

What goes wrong: you open with what you're selling. “Hi, want to buy some candy?” They say no before they have looked at you.

2The Qualifying Question

What makes someone qualified to buy candy? Or water?

They want it. That is the entire test. Qualified doesn't mean rich and it doesn't mean friendly — it means hungry, or thirsty, right now.

“You guys hungry?”

“You guys thirsty?”

“Do you like juice?”

Then you wait. Do not fill the silence. The silence is doing your work.

They answer. What are your options?
Yes — qualified. Go to step 3, and use the word they just gave you.
No — not qualified. Thank them and move on. There are three thousand people on this pier.

Two paths, and no third one. You will learn to handle “no, but…” later — objection handling only makes sense once the frame is automatic. For now a no is a no, and moving on fast is the skill.

What goes wrong: you skip this and present to everyone. Then you're pitching juice to people who aren't thirsty, and you're slow as well as ignored.

3The Presentation

They said they're thirsty. What do you say?

“Great — I've got juice, water and soda. Three dollars. What sounds good?”

Notice what is not in that sentence: candy. They said thirsty, so you sold drinks. The presentation is built out of the answer they just gave you — that is the whole job of this step.

Name the price yourself, plainly, in the same breath. Making them ask is making them work.

Why does step 2 come before this one?

Because you cannot tailor a presentation to someone who hasn't told you anything yet. The qualifying question isn't a filter you run to get rid of people — it is you collecting the material you are about to present with.

They said thirsty → “I've got juice, water and soda…”
They said hungry → “I've got chocolate, gummies and chips…”
They said they like juice → “I've got orange and apple, ice cold…”

Same box, same prices, three different sentences. Use their word, not yours. If they said thirsty, say thirsty back to them.

What goes wrong: the untailored pitch — you run the same list every time, in the same order, no matter what they said. They can hear that it isn't about them, because it isn't. A tailored presentation is the difference between answering a person and reciting inventory at them.

4The Close

They picked the juice. What is the next sentence?

“What's your Venmo?”

You assume the close. Not “do you want one?” — they already answered that in step 2, and asking again invites them to change their mind.

Note that this one is still a question. It just isn't a question about whether. It's a question about how.

What goes wrong: “So… did you want one?” You handed back a decision they had already made.

5The Rehash

Any guesses what rehash means?

Going back for the sale you didn't ask for. They just bought — which makes them the easiest person on the pier to sell to, and most people walk away from them.

“Got any friends who are thirsty? Want to grab something for them?”

Everyone has a sale hidden. One question finds it.

What goes wrong: you take the money and say thanks. It costs one sentence to find out if there was a second sale, and you skipped it.

Now go count.

Knowing the five steps and running them are different things. After every conversation you tap one button — the furthest you got. Talk only, presentation, or close. That gives you a total for everyone you spoke to, and it shows which step is actually costing you people rather than which one you think is.
