One rule over all five — end with a question mark.

You are on the pier with a box of candy and a cooler. Here is the whole script.

    “Hi, how are you?”

    What matters more than the words?

    “Quick question—”

    “Are you guys thirsty?”or“Are you hungry?”

    Then wait.

    in the basket juice · water · snickers · chips · an apple

    No “what do you want?” — the dash runs you straight into the close.

  4. 4

    The Close

    “—what's better for you, cash or Venmo?”

    Same breath as the presentation. They answered “do you want one?” back in step 2.

*  Hover one — or tap, on a phone.

Every asterisk is a line I would not hand over. I ask it instead, and let the answer come back before the script does. A rule you worked out is a rule you keep; a rule you were told is a rule you forget by the second conversation.

Done right it should feel like they are building this with me, not taking it from me. It is closer to motivational interviewing than teaching — I am drawing the answer out of them and letting them arrive at it, so by the end they are running a script they think they wrote. The lines above are where we land, not where we start.

More behind the script — has FUGI, the objections, the impulse curve and the rest.

The five steps are the frame. This is everything that makes them land — read one, go run it, come back.

FFUGI

Four levers. You do not pull all four — you read the person in front of you and pick the one they are already leaning towards.

What are the four?
FFear of loss. The thing goes away. “This is the last cold one.”
UUrgency. The window is now. “I'm heading off the pier in ten minutes.”
GGreed. They come out ahead. “Two for five, instead of three each.”
IIndifference. You don't need the sale.
Why would acting like you don't care ever help?

Because wanting it too much is the thing people can smell, and it is the thing that makes them back away. Indifference is the takeaway:

“Honestly, this might not be for you — do you even like juice?”

You just gave them permission to say no, which is the only reason their yes means anything. It is the hardest of the four to run without sounding rude, and the strongest when it works.

Pulling all four at once reads as desperate. One lever, chosen because you watched them, is the tailoring from step 3 applied to emotion instead of product.

2Objection handling

Step 2 said a no is a no and to move on. That is true while you are learning the frame. Once the frame is automatic, most nos are not nos — they are one specific thing in the way.

“I'm in a rush.” · “How long is this going to take?”

“No worries — takes thirty seconds!”

You did not argue with them. You agreed, then made the cost of hearing you out smaller than the cost of getting away from you.

“I don't have any cash.”

“Great, nobody does — we've got a QR code. Do you have your phone?”

“Great” is doing the work. The objection they thought would end it turns out to be the most normal thing you have heard all day, so it stops being an exit.

“It's too expensive.”

“Yeah, it's not free — that's why it's two for five instead of three each. Which two do you want?”

Never defend the price and never drop it. Agree that it costs money, then give them a version of the deal that is better than the one they were refusing.

“I'm too busy.”

“Same — I'll be quick. You hungry?”

Match their speed instead of asking them to slow down, and go straight back to the qualifying question. Busy people will answer one question. They will not stand through a pitch.

The rest of the ones you'll hear all day

Same three moves every time. Read the reply, not just the objection — each one agrees, shrinks it, and ends on a question.

“I'm not interested.” “All good — you're not thirsty at all?”
“Maybe later.” “For sure, I'm here till four. Want me to hold you a cold one?”
“I have to ask my friend.” “Of course — is she thirsty too?”
“I already have one.” “Nice. Is it still cold?”
“I'm just walking through.” “So is everyone — takes ten seconds. You hungry?”
“Is this a scam?” “Fair. It's candy, it's three dollars. Want one?”

Notice none of them argue, and none of them are longer than the objection was. If your reply is longer than their complaint, you have started defending yourself.

What do all of those have in common?

Three moves, every time:

Agree. Never argue. “No worries.” “Great.”
Shrink it. Make the obstacle smaller than they think it is.
Ask. Hand the next move back to them.

That third one is the rule from Learn, still running. Every reply ends in a question mark, or you have handed them a statement and a silence.

Worth knowing: “no cash” is exactly what an N is in the tracker. Every N you log is a conversation this card would have turned into a $.

3The Impulse Curve

Their willingness to buy is not flat. It climbs while you talk, peaks, and then falls.

Where do you close?

At the peak. The intro raises it a little, the short story raises it more, the presentation takes it to the top — and that is the moment you ask for the money.

Below the buying line nobody buys, however good the product is. Above it, they will. Everything before the close is you getting them over that line, and the close is you spending it before it drains.

They said yes. What do you do with the fall on the other side?

That fall is buyer's remorse, and it starts the second the money moves. The answer is not to leave.

You stay and chat. Keep talking to them, keep building the relationship, be a person rather than someone who came for their three dollars and left with it. The sale holds because they like you afterwards, not only because they wanted it beforehand.

It is also where the rehash lives — you are already standing there talking, which is the natural moment to ask who else is thirsty.

The curve is flat and they're going. What lifts it?

Voice inflection. The curve tracks your energy more than your words. Flat delivery flattens them; lift at the end of a sentence and they come with you. It is the fastest lever you have and it costs nothing.

Along the bottom of the curve is the rest of it: SEE at the intro, your qualifying questions through the middle, and hotspots — whatever they lit up about, which is what you come back to when you need the curve to climb again.

4Pitch · Pace · Attitude

Three inputs. Your numbers at the end of a shift tell you which one was off.

Lots of conversations, almost no sales. What broke?

Your pitch. The pace is there — you are talking to everyone — and it is not converting, which means the words are wrong, or they are not tailored to what people told you.

Barely any conversations at all. What broke?

Your pace. You are standing still between people, picking who looks friendly, and spending too long on the ones who already said no.

You know the pitch, you're moving, and it's still dead.

Your attitude. It is the one you cannot see from inside, and the one that shows up first — in the two E's of SEE, and in the shape of the impulse curve. Fix it and the same words start working.

These three are not a checklist you tick off one at a time. They are a ratio, which is the next card.

5The Kool-Aid Mix

The last card told you which of the three is short. This one is why you cannot fix it by doing more of whichever one you are already good at.

Three things go in the jug. Which is which?
MMix is your pitch. The substance. Nothing happens without it — and on its own it is not a drink.
WWater is your pace. The volume you move through. It is what carries the pitch to anybody at all.
SSugar is your attitude. What makes the whole thing go down.

Three ingredients, one drink. Nobody drinks an ingredient.

So what does each wrong ratio taste like?
Powder — all pitch, no pace. You know exactly what to say and you say it to four people in an afternoon. Perfect words, no water to carry them.
Bitter water — all pace, weak pitch. A hundred conversations and nothing in any of them worth stopping for. Lots of talks, one or two sales.
Bitter either way — no sugar. Right words, right speed, dead delivery. You have technically run a sale and nobody wants it.
Sweet water — all three, in proportion.
Your numbers are bad. Do you add more mix?

Only if mix is what is short. Adding mix to bitter water does not fix bitter water — it makes powder. Talking to fewer people with a better pitch is how a slow day becomes a dead one.

This is the mistake almost everybody makes: they get bad numbers and do more of the ingredient they are already best at, because it is the comfortable one. Find the one that is short, and add that.

Nobody's problem is that they don't know the five steps. It is that they run them like a script with no sugar in it, or they run their personality and skip the process. The ratio is the whole job.

Reading your marks: a pile of / is usually sugar — they did not turn down your words, they never heard them. A pile of Q is usually mix. Barely any marks at all is water.

6The Gumball Theory

Ten machines, ten quarters. How do you spend them?

“Best chance statistically is to put all of your quarters in one machine.”

The machine is your pitch. Run the same one, over and over, and every conversation is another quarter into the same machine — so the numbers coming out of it actually mean something.

Change your words every time and you have not run one experiment forty times. You have run forty experiments once each, and you have changed the statistics of it working. You cannot tell whether the pitch is bad or whether that one guy was in a hurry, because nothing was held still long enough to measure.

So when are you allowed to change it?

After enough quarters to know. Change one thing, run it the same number of times, compare. That is a different machine, tested properly — not fidgeting mid-shift because the last two said no.

Two nos in a row feels like evidence. It is not. It is two quarters.

This is why the tracker exists. A close rate only means something if the pitch stayed still underneath it. Hold the pitch, log the marks, then change one thing on purpose.

7The Marks

How a shift gets logged on paper: one mark beside each conversation, written the second it ends. The tracker is this legend with the arithmetic done for you.

Why it matters: a tally of sales tells you how you did. A tally of how each one ended tells you what to change. Five X's is a different shift from five Q's, and they need opposite fixes.
