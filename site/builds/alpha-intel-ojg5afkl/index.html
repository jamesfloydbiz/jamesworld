§ 01 — The companyStructure & control

It is a holding company with schools attached

No official org chart exists publicly. This is reconstructed from Dan Meyer, Bucks County Beacon, Wikipedia and Colossus. Critics call it a Liemandt-controlled keiretsu, and the structure itself is the controversy.

The loop

  └─ owns the for-profit vendors:
      2 Hour Learning — the platform · Crossover — all hiring

The critique, stated plainly. Non-profit schools contract services from for-profit vendors owned and managed by the same people — 100% of Unbound Academy's named board is affiliated with the vendors. Know it cold, and never take a job whose brief is defending it.

What the product actually is

  • "AI" here means adaptive learning apps — IXL / Khan-style — not large language models. The branding outruns the tech, and that gap is where most of the criticism lives.
  • 2 Hour Learning is the real product. Alpha is the flagship storefront; the engine is licensed across every sister school. "Alpha Anywhere" plus the virtual map says the play is software distribution — the buildings are demos.
  • Guides, not teachers. Humans do motivation and life skills; the software does academics. Hired for coaching, not credentials — ex-LA Galaxy director of education, a Harvard learning-design master's, mindset coaches from Nike and the NFL.
  • The stated end state: the model out to a billion kids on a sub-$1,000 tablet. The front door costs $40,000 a year.

How the math works

  • Revenue = published tuition × students per campus.
  • Costs, estimated: guides = (students ÷ ~7) × ~$110k; fixed leadership and ops ~$450k per campus; rent ~$400k per campus; platform and G&A ~15% of revenue.
  • The loop, in the P&L: the non-profit schools pay the for-profit vendors, so that "platform and G&A" line is essentially the take flowing back to Liemandt's entities.

§ 04 — The gauntletHow they hire

Roughly one in a thousand gets through

All hiring routes through Crossover. MacKenzie Price puts the guide bar at about 0.1% of candidates — "like getting into Harvard" — against 80,000 applicants last year, at $100k+.

The five steps to the title

#StepWhat it actually tests
01CCAT — 85th percentile or higher, twice, the second proctored50 questions in 15 minutes. Pass bar 35/50. Verbal ~34%, numerical ~34%, spatial ~22%, logic ~10%. Spatial is where most people die. No calculator; switching tabs disqualifies.
02Essays on coaching others to high standardsReflection, real experience, clarity, conviction. Long written prompts.
03The Trojan Horse videoTell the story of the Trojan Horse as if to a room of kids. No slides, no gimmicks. Reviewed by Crossover HR, the hiring team, and an AI pass.
04Interviews with a Lead Guide or Head of K-8They are hunting for "the spark" — can you light up a room of kids and adults.
05Shadow day — only ~5% reach itA full day onsite, watched closely. Can you make a child feel seen within minutes? Command attention without controlling? Are students begging you to come back? ~60–70% pass.
The Trojan Horse is the whole application in one artifact. It is a required step and a perfectly shareable piece of work — do it in public, before they ask, and it doubles as the cold-email hook and the centre of any launch video. This is the highest-leverage single thing on this page.

§ 05 — Parents' roomLive info session

What they tell parents when the room is full

From the live session, with my margin notes. These are the numbers they volunteer — which makes them the numbers they are comfortable defending.

  • 97% re-enrolment. Their claim
  • 80,000 applicants for guide roles last year. Guides hold a bachelor's; credentials are not required — they are hired for coaching.
  • Ratios: ~1:5 elementary, ~1:10 middle, ~1:15 high school.
  • New campuses open with 40–50 students (SF ~60), and they will not open a campus until 25 are enrolled. My margin note: "So 25 × price."
  • $100 application fee, rolling admissions, and a shadow day is required to apply — for the kid, not just the adult.
  • Mastery-based, 90%+ to advance, no letter grades. About 50 total graduates so far — "all became entrepreneurs or went to their top-choice university." Their claim
  • Subject and learning specialists are a national remote team; reading specialists are on campus. My margin note: "this is the tutors from other places potentially."
  • Alpha Anywhere (homeschool) covers grades 3–8. They license the engine to private schools, do not franchise, and are not taking new licensees right now.
  • "Radical transparency" — their words — about parent surveys, MAP tests and student survey results.
The internal currency. One student testimonial on their own site: "we have our own currency that motivates us to do more work." Kids earn something for doing work. It is the motivation engine in miniature and the most debatable design choice in the model — worth having a real opinion about before anyone asks.
§ 06 — The readTensions & sentiment

Where the fire is

Polarised: ecstatic parents at one end, burned parents and ed-world critics at the other, a thoughtful skeptical middle. The press turned noticeably harder across 2025–26 at the WIRED and 404 inflection.

What holds up

  • The proof point isn't the test scores — it's that kids beg to skip summer break.
  • Afternoons are the real differentiator: leadership, entrepreneurship, financial literacy, public speaking, fencing, robotics.
  • Alpha High "masterpiece projects" are genuinely extraordinary — one student raised $350K and built Texas' biggest mountain-bike park; another made a cancer documentary seen 4.5M times.
  • Scott Alexander's ACX review — the most balanced long-read — lands in the middle: skeptical of the extraordinary claims, but found it more real than pure critics allow. Outcome tracks student fit.

What burns

  • The #1 recurring complaint: parents find out their kid fell behind months after it happened. The dashboard didn't surface it, or they trusted it too long.
  • Brownsville. Around six families raised concerns in 2023–24; the most vocal said kids were "so stressed out" while the school used them as evidence the model works. WIRED surfaced 9-year-olds denied snacks until they hit metrics.
  • Surveillance. Webcams — at home too, unless a parent opts out — plus eye, mouse and scroll tracking. The top concern among cited experts.
  • IXL deactivated their account for a terms violation and stated its product is not a replacement for trained, caring teachers. That one cuts at the core pitch.
  • AI lesson quality. Internal documents flagged AI-generated lessons as sometimes doing "more harm than good."
Two traps to avoid. First, most Glassdoor material is for "Alpha Public Schools," a separate California charter network — do not attribute it to this Alpha. Second, the posture that draws the most anger is "it either works for your child or it doesn't." If a role's brief is defending the governance or the surveillance rather than fixing the trust, that's the line.

The three tensions, stated as questions

  • The dream is a billion kids on a cheap tablet. The front door is $40,000 a year. Brownsville and the public-school push are the stated answer — does it hold?
  • Kids earn currency for doing work. Motivation, or paying children to learn?
  • Results are real but internal. Who verifies them, and what happens the first time someone independent tries?
