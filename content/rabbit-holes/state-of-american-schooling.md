How many kids

There are roughly 60 million young people in school in the United States between the ages of about three and eighteen.¹ That figure is larger than the number most often quoted, because it counts preschool, private schools and homeschools alongside the public system rather than only the public system on its own.

Where they go to school

About 80% of them attend traditional public schools, and another 7% or so attend charter schools.² The remainder are in private schools or homeschools, with private schools making up the great majority of that group.³

The four-way split is a derivation rather than something any single federal dataset reports, because every federal count places charters inside "public." It is also probably conservative at the bottom end: homeschooling alone runs 5-6% and private schooling 8-10%, which puts the two together nearer 13-16% than 10%.³

What matters more than the shares is the direction they are moving. Between 2019 and 2024, district enrollment fell 4.4%, a loss of more than 1.9 million students. Over the same period charter enrollment grew 14.7%, a gain of 492,000. Homeschool enrollment grew another 4.9% in 2024-25, double its pre-pandemic rate, and 36% of reporting states hit their highest homeschool enrollment on record. Private enrollment grew 28% against pre-pandemic numbers on the Census household survey, though the school-based federal survey shows far less growth and the two instruments disagree by 2.3 million students.⁴

![Where students went, 2019 to 2024 — district enrollment down 4.4%, charter up 14.7%, homeschool up 4.9% a year, private up 28%](/charts/schooling/enrollment-shift.svg)
One shrinking bar and three growing ones. This is the clearest single picture of the demand shift.

Where the money comes from

Total government funding for public preK-12 education is now just over $1 trillion a year. Roughly 88% of it comes from state and local government and about 12% from the federal government, which makes sense given that states and districts make almost all of the operating decisions.⁵ That money moves through roughly 13,000 school districts sitting under 50 state legislatures and 50 state education agencies.⁶

For traditional public schools the split runs about 43% local, 45% state and 12% federal, with property taxes supplying nearly two thirds of the local share. The federal portion varies far more by state than the headline suggests, from 22.7% in Mississippi to 6.3% in New Jersey.⁷

![Where the money comes from — local 43%, state 45%, federal 12%, with the federal share ranging from 22.7% in Mississippi to 6.3% in New Jersey](/charts/schooling/funding-sources.svg)
The federal share is the smallest and the most variable.

Charter schools draw on the same public money in the form of per-pupil payments, but in most states receive little or no facilities funding and must raise the rest themselves.⁸ Private schools are funded mostly by tuition, and homeschools are funded by the home.

One live change here is education savings accounts and vouchers. Vouchers direct tax money that would have gone to a district toward another institution at a parent's choosing, and ESAs are state-funded accounts that parents apply for annually and spend on their child's education. Iowa pays $8,148 for 2026-27; Texas launched at up to roughly $10,000 and drew more than 100,000 applications in under two weeks; Arizona passed 100,000 enrolled in January 2026.¹⁰

A century of spending

Spending has risen almost without interruption for a hundred years. In 1919-20, current expenditure per pupil was $664 in today's money. By 2022-23 it was $17,553 — roughly 26 times the real 1920 figure.⁹

![A century of spending per pupil — $664 in 1919-20 rising to $17,553 in 2022-23 in constant dollars](/charts/schooling/spending-century.svg)
Two multi-year pauses interrupt the climb, in the early 1990s and again from 2009 to 2013, both following recessions.

The pandemic relief money is the interesting non-event here. ESSER produced no visible spike in this measure: real per-pupil spending rose 3.7% in 2020-21, 1.7% in 2021-22, then fell 0.1% in 2022-23, because $190 billion spread over four years is about 6% of annual spending and much of it went to capital projects that this measure does not count.⁹

Where the money goes

Current spending per pupil reached $17,619 in FY2024, up 6.6% in a year, and it varies enormously by place. New York State runs $31,918 per pupil and DC $31,529, while Idaho runs $11,060 and Utah $11,347.¹¹ Among the largest districts, New York City spends $35,796 per pupil and Los Angeles Unified $25,631.¹²

Schools spend about 79% of their money on people — roughly 55% on salaries and 24% on benefits — with contracted services like transportation, food and janitorial work the next largest category at around 11%.¹³

![Where the money goes — salaries 55%, benefits 24%, purchased services 11%, everything else 10%, against other people-delivered sectors](/charts/schooling/where-money-goes.svg)
79% is not unusual. It is where every people-delivered service sits.

That last point is worth holding onto, because 79% is often quoted as though it were evidence of bloat. The OECD reports staff compensation at 79% of current expenditure across member countries, so the US sits exactly at the international average, and every service delivered by humans lands in the same band. Retail runs near 10% because it buys inventory instead of labour; restaurants about 33%; hospitals about 55%; home care 65-75%; police and fire 85-90%.¹³

What has actually driven spending up is headcount rather than pay. Staff per student has risen more than 70% since 1970 and teachers per student nearly 40%, while real teacher wages fell 6.2% over the last thirty years.¹⁴ Salary costs grow because schools employ more adults per child, not because each adult costs more in real terms.

How students score

There is no standardised measure of what American students know. More than fifty different tests are in use across the states, the methodologies behind them are contested, and there is no research establishing what the ceiling of children's ability actually is, because we measure what children do rather than what they could do.¹⁵ ¹⁶ What follows uses international assessments instead, which at least compare like with like across systems.

These tests sample differently. PISA samples by age, testing 15-year-olds; TIMSS and PIRLS sample by years of schooling.¹⁷

PIRLS tests reading in the fourth year of schooling. In 2021 the US scored 548 against a scale centrepoint of 500, higher than 24 of the 32 comparable systems and lower than three.¹⁸

TIMSS tests mathematics and science in the fourth and eighth years. In 2023 US fourth-graders scored 517 in maths — down 18 points from 2019 and the lowest since 1995 — ranking 22nd of 63 systems, in a field running from 362 in South Africa to 615 in Singapore.¹⁹ In science they scored 532, ranking 12th of 63.²⁰ Eighth-grade maths came in at 488, down 27 points from 2019, ranking 22nd of 44.²¹

PISA tests 15-year-olds. In 2022 US maths was 465 against an OECD average of 472, ranking 26th of 81 and the lowest US maths score in the history of the assessment.²² Reading was 504 against an average of 476, ranking 6th of 81.²³ Science was 499, ranking 10th.²⁴

![How US students score — PIRLS 548, TIMSS grade 4 maths 517 and science 532, TIMSS grade 8 maths 488, PISA maths 465, reading 504, science 499](/charts/schooling/test-scores.svg)
Reading is the one subject that clears the line. Mathematics is the one that does not.

What sticks into adulthood

PIAAC tests adults aged 16 to 65. In 2023 the US scored 258 in literacy against an international average of 260, 249 in numeracy against 263, and 247 in adaptive problem solving against 251.²⁵ Ranked strictly, that puts the US around 25th of 31 in numeracy and close to average in literacy.

The distribution is worse than the average. 34.2% of US adults now sit at or below Level 1 in numeracy, the fifth-highest share of the 31 countries tested, up from 29% in 2017.²⁶ For scale, Finland leads on everything with 296 in literacy, 294 in numeracy and 276 in problem solving, while Chile sits at 218 in literacy.²⁷

Young adults are not carrying the country upward. The 16-24 band scored 261 in literacy, ranking 22nd of 31, and 248 in numeracy, ranking 29th of 31 — better than their elders at reading and materially worse at maths.²⁸

Across every international assessment, US results either declined from the previous measurement or held flat. On none of them, at any age, did the US improve significantly.²⁹

Who actually lost the score

There is a pattern inside the averages worth naming, and it is not surprising: the losses are concentrated at the bottom.

On TIMSS fourth-grade maths, students at the 10th percentile fell 37 points between 2019 and 2023 while scores at the 90th percentile showed no measurable change. The gap between them widened by 35 points in maths and 26 in science, and the US 10th-percentile fourth-grade maths score was the lowest in the assessment's roughly thirty-year history.³⁰

![Who actually lost the score — TIMSS grade 4 maths, the 90th percentile flat while the 10th fell 37 points and the gap widened 35](/charts/schooling/distribution.svg)
The average hides this. The top held and the bottom fell out.

Two things complicate the clean version of that story. At eighth grade the pattern reverses in maths, where top performers fell 29 points against 19 for low performers. And over the longer run the top is falling too, just more slowly: since 2012, thirteen-year-olds' maths dropped 7 points at the 90th percentile against 27 at the 10th.³⁰

Time in school

Time in school varies by state but averages around 178 days and 1,227 hours in session, of which roughly 1,000 hours is actual instruction.³¹ Colorado sets the lowest day count at 160 and Kansas the highest at 186, while hour minimums run from 720 in Arizona to 1,260 in Texas.³² Districts decide how much of that time counts as instructional, which recess, assemblies and other draws all reduce.

Close to half of instructional time goes to reading, writing, mathematics and science alone.³³ Other countries distribute the same total differently — Japan and Israel run more days at fewer hours each, closer to 200 days at around four hours.³⁴

Put end to end, a child who reaches eighteen has been awake for about 105,120 hours, assuming eight hours of sleep a night. Roughly 20,579 of those hours went to school and schoolwork. That is just under 20% of a waking childhood.³⁵

![The time a childhood pays — 20,579 hours of school out of 105,120 waking hours before turning 18](/charts/schooling/the-20-percent.svg)
Roughly a fifth of a childhood, spent inside one system.

Breaks matter to learning, so it is worth knowing that no national dataset on recess exists at all. Survey self-report puts elementary recess at 25-30 minutes a day, and only about ten states mandate a minimum, commonly 20 minutes.³⁶ The absence of the data is itself the finding.

Chronic absenteeism

Almost a quarter of American students are not reliably showing up. Chronic absenteeism, defined as missing 10% or more of school days, sat at 15% in 2018-19. It hit 28.3% in 2022, then fell to 25.7%, 23.5% and 22.6% across the following three years.³⁸ In roughly half of large urban districts, more than 30% of students are chronically absent.

![Chronic absenteeism — 15% in 2018-19, peaking at 28.3% in 2022 and settling at 22.6% in 2024-25](/charts/schooling/absenteeism.svg)
It is not returning to baseline.

Absence correlates with lower achievement in PISA's own background data, and the causation plausibly runs both ways: a student who is disengaged attends less, and a student who attends less falls further behind.³⁷

Teachers

Teacher salaries are the largest line in the budget and teaching practice is the largest lever on outcomes, so both deserve a look.

Roughly 77% of teachers are women, and the average age is 43 with about 14 years of experience.³⁹ The age distribution is often described as bimodal; what the data actually shows is a thinning middle, with teachers at 3-9 years of experience falling from 33% to 29% between 2011-12 and 2020-21 while those past twenty years rose from 23% to 26%.³⁹

Average starting salary is $48,112, and the national average sits around $65,090.⁴⁰ Against comparable college graduates, though, teachers earn 17.1% less in total compensation and 25.2% less in wages alone — 74.8 cents on the dollar in 2025, down from 93.9 cents in 1996. The penalty exists in every state, from 10.4% in Rhode Island to 40.7% in Colorado.⁴¹

![The teacher pay penalty — teacher weekly wages fell from 93.9 cents to 74.8 cents per dollar earned by comparable college graduates between 1996 and 2025](/charts/schooling/pay-penalty.svg)
Thirty years, one direction.

About one in eight teaching positions is now filled by someone not fully certified or is vacant outright: 365,967 teachers not fully certified plus 45,582 vacancies in 2025. Schools serving the most students of colour are four times as likely to employ an uncertified teacher.⁴²

Certification is a weaker signal than it looks. What predicts student results for traditional teachers is subject-matter knowledge and years in the classroom rather than licences and degrees, and randomised studies of alternative-route teachers found they matched or beat certified teachers in maths. That is not the same as saying undercertification does not matter — studies in North Carolina and New York City find teachers on emergency credentials produce smaller gains, though they are also assigned harder classrooms. The defensible claim is narrower than the popular one: certification route predicts little, which is different from certification predicting nothing.⁴³

Turnover runs about 16% a year, with 8% leaving teaching entirely — a rate statistically unchanged since 2012-13. Vacancy sits near 1.4%, which is a healthy rate, and replacing a teacher costs between $12,000 and $25,000. Turnover runs 35-37% higher in the highest-poverty schools.⁴⁴ ⁴⁵

Phonics, and what Mississippi did

Mississippi passed its Literacy-Based Promotion Act in 2013. It funded statewide literacy coaching, required teacher training, and added K-3 screening and third-grade retention — phonics was one component of a package rather than the whole policy. The state ranked 49th of 50 in NAEP fourth-grade reading in 2013, 29th by 2019 and roughly 8th or 9th by 2024, while fourth-grade proficiency rose 11 points as the national average fell 4.⁴⁶

![Mississippi's climb — NAEP grade 4 reading rank, 49th in 2013 to 29th in 2019 to roughly 8th by 2024, with gains flattening after 2022](/charts/schooling/mississippi-naep.svg)
Causal estimates put the real effect near 5 NAEP points, far below what the ranking jump implies.

Since then, 43 states and DC have passed laws or adopted policies on evidence-based reading instruction.⁴⁷ The results are not uniform. Mississippi's own gains stalled after 2022, and Florida, Arizona and Nebraska changed parts of their reading instruction and still saw scores fall.⁴⁶ ⁴⁷ Mississippi funded coaching, training, screening and follow-through; states that passed a mandate and stopped there got less.

Grade inflation

Grades have separated from performance. ACT analysed more than four million students across roughly 4,800 schools and found that between 2016 and 2021 average GPA rose from 3.22 to 3.39 while the average ACT composite fell from 20.8 to 20.3.⁴⁸

![Grades up, scores down — GPA rising from 3.22 to 3.39 between 2016 and 2021 while the ACT composite fell from 20.8 to 20.3](/charts/schooling/grade-inflation.svg)
The grade went up while the measured skill went down.

NAEP transcript data shows the same thing over a longer window: graduates averaged 3.11 GPA in 2019, up from 3.00 in 2009 and 2.68 in 1990, while twelfth-grade NAEP maths declined. Said plainly, an A student today performs worse than an A student a decade ago. By 2022 more than 89% of high schoolers received an A or B in maths, English, social studies and science. The inflation was more pronounced in higher-poverty schools and among lower-income students.⁴⁸ ⁴⁹

AI and devices

The average school district accessed 2,982 distinct edtech tools over the 2024-25 year, up nearly 9% year over year — though individual usage is far smaller, at 48 tools per student and 50 per educator.⁵⁰

AI adoption estimates vary widely by source and method. RAND put teacher use at 53% in 2024-25, double the prior year. The Center for Democracy and Technology put it at 85% of teachers and 86% of students. Pew found 64% of teens use AI chatbots and 54% use them for schoolwork.⁵¹ On effectiveness the picture is more sober: a 2025 review of 28 studies covering nearly 5,000 K-12 students found positive effects from AI tutoring but a negligible advantage over non-AI tutoring using the same active-learning approach, and a meta-analysis found effects fall to 0.08 in interventions running a semester or longer.⁵¹

On hardware, 38 states and DC now require districts to ban or restrict phone use, while one-to-one laptop programmes run at 78% of districts for K-2, 84% for grades 3-5, 90% for middle school and 88% for high school — up from 43%, 69% and 66% in 2020.⁵² ⁵³

![Devices in, phones out — one-to-one device adoption by grade band in 2020 and 2026, alongside 38 states restricting phones](/charts/schooling/devices.svg)
One screen goes out and another stays in every hand.

The enrollment cliff

The last thing to look at is supply and demand. There are fewer children now than a few years ago, and the trend continues downward in the US and almost everywhere else.

The US total fertility rate fell to 1.599 in 2024, an all-time low, against a replacement rate of 2.1. Births actually rose 1% that year even as the rate fell, because the population of childbearing-age women grew — largely through immigration.⁵⁵ South Korea sits at 0.75, roughly 64% below replacement.⁵⁴ Between 2010 and 2020 the US under-five population fell from 20.2 million to 18.4 million.⁵⁶

Staffing has moved the other way. The pupil-teacher ratio fell from 26.9 in 1955 to about 14.7 today, though that is a pupil-teacher ratio rather than a class size — it counts special-education and other non-classroom teachers, and actual average class sizes run nearer 19 in self-contained elementary and 21-22 at middle and high school.⁵⁷ The research on class size supports smaller classes at the low end, where Tennessee's randomised Project STAR found real gains moving from 22-25 students to 13-17, with the largest effects for low-income and minority students. Hanushek's review of roughly 300 estimates finds no average effect. Both can be true, because policy rarely operates anywhere near STAR's dose.⁵⁸

Public school enrollment peaked at 50.8 million in fall 2019 and had fallen to 49.4 million by fall 2024, a loss of more than 1.4 million students. NCES projects an 8% decrease between 2019 and 2030, with public enrollment below 47 million by 2030-31, and the decline is geographically concentrated — California and New York project roughly −15% by 2031 while Florida and Texas grow.⁵⁹ ⁶⁰

![The enrollment cliff — public school enrollment falling from 50.8 million in 2019 to 49.4 million in 2024, projected below 47 million by 2030](/charts/schooling/enrollment-cliff.svg)
California and New York project roughly −15% by 2031. Florida and Texas grow.

Students are revenue. A district losing whole percentage points of enrollment has to cut whole percentage points of expense, which is considerably harder when the costs are buildings and pensions. At the same time the share of students who are English learners or have disabilities keeps rising, which raises the cost per student in exactly the districts under the most pressure.⁶¹

This is a few years out for the US, which is why what is happening in Asia matters. Japan has been below replacement since 1974 and now closes roughly 450 schools a year, about 1.2 every day.⁶² In Korea, 210 elementary schools enrolled zero first-graders in 2026, up 81% in five years; first-grade enrollment fell 15.5% in two years to below 300,000 for the first time; total K-12 is projected below 4 million by 2031 against a 1993 peak of 8.82 million.⁶³

What I take from this

Reading all of it back, most of it is negative. As an optimist and an entrepreneur, I read it as opportunity.

The first thing I keep returning to is that time is a tax, and it is paid at a flat rate. Every child hands over roughly a fifth of their waking childhood to one system.³⁵ What differs is the return, and the return collapses fastest at the bottom: the students at the 10th percentile lost 37 points on fourth-grade maths while the top of the distribution did not measurably move.³⁰ Families with means buy their way to a better return through private schooling and tutoring. Everyone pays the same twenty percent; not everyone gets the same thing back for it.⁶⁴

The second is that the receipts we hand parents no longer describe the product. GPAs rose while measured performance fell, and the inflation was worse in higher-poverty schools and among lower-income students.⁴⁸ ⁴⁹ The families least equipped to verify a grade independently were handed the least accurate signal. That is the part that bothers me most.

The third is that learning science can move practice at national scale, because it just did. Mississippi's results moved 43 states and DC to change reading policy inside a decade.⁴⁶ ⁴⁷ But traction and outcomes are different things. Mississippi's own gains stalled after 2022, and Florida, Arizona and Nebraska changed instruction and still saw scores fall. Mississippi funded coaching, training, screening and follow-through. States that passed a mandate and stopped there got less. Implementation is the variable, not the finding.

The fourth is a myth this data kills. Teachers are not among the worst-paid professions in America. Average pay of $65,090 and starting pay of $48,112 both sit above the US median individual wage.⁴⁰ The 25% penalty is real, but it is measured against comparable college graduates, not against all workers.⁴¹ So teachers are paid roughly average for a worker and poorly for someone with a degree. Those are two different claims, and public argument runs them together constantly.

The fifth is the structural one: the system has no quality feedback loop, which means it cannot reliably improve, because it is not reliably measured. State tests have exactly the wrong incentives — under No Child Left Behind, states set cut scores low enough to report 80% proficient while NAEP found 30% of the same students proficient. NAEP is the instrument that exposes that gap rather than the thing distorting it; it is federally administered and deliberately independent of the states for precisely this reason. But NAEP cannot close the loop on its own. Its achievement levels have been criticised by the National Academies, the National Academy of Education and the GAO; it carries no consequences; and it samples rather than testing every child, so no parent learns anything about their own kid from it.¹⁶ ⁶⁸

The sixth is a matter of timing. On birthrates the US is roughly a generation behind Korea and Japan, which puts the years around 2028 to 2035 as the window when enrollment decay starts producing the effects already visible in Asia.⁵⁵ ⁵⁹ ⁶⁰ ⁶² ⁶³ That makes the coming decade the most volatile stretch in the history of American schooling, and volatility is when a market becomes willing to accept a better answer.

The seventh is that people are already leaving, in three different ways. They leave the building, through chronic absence now at 22%. They leave the district, through charter enrollment up 492,000 while district enrollment fell 1.9 million. They leave the system entirely, through homeschooling at all-time highs in a third of reporting states.⁴ ³⁸ ⁷⁰

Stated dissatisfaction is a weaker signal than that, and worth quoting precisely. Gallup finds 35% of all Americans satisfied with the quality of US K-12 education, down 8 points in a single year and the lowest across 26 years of polling. Parent satisfaction with their own child's education is a different number entirely — 74%, essentially at its 26-year average.⁷¹ People are far more critical of the system than of the school their own child attends. Revealed preference is the more reliable evidence, and the enrollment numbers already supply it.⁷²

The last is that the tools are new. The science of how people learn is not new — Ebbinghaus published the forgetting curve in 1885 — but its application to schooling is. The National Reading Panel reported in 2000 and states did not begin acting until 2013.⁷³ A field where the best available findings are more than a decade ahead of practice is a field with a lot of unclaimed ground in it.

Birthrates are falling and student capability is declining. Both of those make the system more malleable than it has been in a century. What got us here cannot get us out — but it does mean the thing is finally movable.
